What to Check Before You Message Anyone

10 Minutes. Do This Every Time.
The Simple Rule

A generic message reads: "I'm a cybersecurity graduate looking for opportunities." It gets ignored. A specific one reads: "I saw your SOC is hiring L1 analysts across two shifts. I've already run a live Sentinel environment solo and closed ~20 real intrusion attempts in the first hour." Same person, completely different reply rate. The difference is ten minutes of research before you type.

  1. 01
    Open their LinkedIn profile. Click "Activity."
    Look for anything about hiring, SOC expansion, a new client win, a breach response, or a conference talk. If a SOC Manager just posted "we're growing the team" or shared a detection-engineering article, that is your opening line. If they reshared an AISA or BSides post, Shino can reference being part of the same community.
  2. 02
    Go to their website. Look for SOC, MDR and compliance language.
    For an MSSP, check whether they advertise a 24/7 SOC, Microsoft Sentinel, or MDR. If they run Sentinel, that is a direct match to Shino's lab and goes in line one. For an internal team in health or finance, look for ISO 27001, NIST CSF, or APRA CPS 234 language, then mirror it. Naming the same framework they care about signals you understand their world.
  3. 03
    Check their jobs on SEEK and LinkedIn.
    Three scenarios, three angles. (1) An L1 SOC role open 4+ weeks means they are struggling to fill it, so lead with "I can triage from day one." (2) Hiring seniors but not juniors means pitch yourself as the cheaper analyst who frees their seniors from monitoring. (3) Not hiring in security at all means go peer-to-peer for intel and referrals, not a direct ask.
  4. 04
    Google the company name + "breach" or "security" + 2025/2026.
    A recent incident, a compliance penalty, or a new security hire announcement all mean budget is moving toward detection right now. If a finance or health employer had an incident, they are rebuilding monitoring, and a SIEM-fluent analyst is exactly who they need. If you find nothing, fall back to the structural pain: regulated data, thin security bench, growing attack surface.
  5. 05
    Write one sentence before you open LinkedIn to write the message.
    Answer this on paper: What is happening at this company right now that means they need someone who can do what Shino does? For example: "They run a Sentinel SOC and can't fill L1 shifts." Or: "They had a breach and are rebuilding detection." Or: "They hold patient data and have no dedicated monitoring." That sentence becomes the first line of the message. Not a paragraph at the end.

The Rules

What Works. What Kills the Message.
✓ Do This
  • Reference something specific about their company or situation in line one. Every single time.
  • Match the message to the audience. SOC Manager = the staffing and triage pain. Internal security lead = the monitoring gap and your automation instinct. Health or finance manager = the data risk, in their words, no jargon.
  • Use one number per message — the ~20 attack attempts closed in the first hour, the 2 Sentinel dashboards, or the self-written KQL detections. Each one tells the whole story.
  • Lead with the live, self-built SOC when the company runs Sentinel or an MDR. That's the differentiator no other candidate at this level has.
  • Connect first, DM 2–3 days later. A person who accepted your request is already warmer than a cold DM.
  • Point to the GitHub and portfolio once, briefly. Verifiable proof is the whole advantage — don't bury it, don't over-sell it.
  • Follow up once at day 7. Then mark as dead and move on. Two messages maximum.
✗ Never This
  • "I hope this message finds you well" or "I am passionate about cybersecurity." Delete. Every time.
  • Listing every tool — Sentinel, Azure, KQL, Python, PowerShell, Wireshark, Nmap, n8n — in the message. Pick what's relevant to them. The rest goes on the resume.
  • Leading with "I am currently looking for opportunities." This centres you, not them. Their problem comes first.
  • Sending the same message to 50 people with only the name changed. People talk.
  • Apologising for being a graduate or calling yourself "junior". The work speaks. Running a live SIEM solo is not junior behaviour, so don't label it that way.
  • Sending a third follow-up. It signals desperation, not persistence.
  • Overstating your work rights. If the role needs a clearance or PR you don't have, that surfaces fast — be accurate about your visa from the first message.
